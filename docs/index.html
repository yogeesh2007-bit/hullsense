1 · The problem

Crewed modules get struck by micrometeoroids and debris. Today, finding out where a strike hit, whether it breached the pressure wall, and how bad the leak is takes hours to months.

ISS Zvezda, 2019–2020
The leak was noticed in 2019. The crew finally found the crack in 2020 by releasing tea leaves and watching where they drifted. NASA's Inspector General later rated the Zvezda leak at its highest risk level.
Shenzhou-20, Nov 2025
Debris cracked the capsule's window while it was docked to Tiangong. Officials said "it is unclear when the damage occurred". The capsule was declared unsafe and the crew came home in a different vehicle.
Soyuz MS-22, Dec 2022
Coolant leaked through a hole of about 0.8 mm, attributed to a likely micrometeoroid. It was found only after crew saw particles spraying out, then located using camera inspection.
ISS, 2004
"Astronauts on the ISS determined that there was an air leak, and they worked for several days before locating it."

The gap HULLSENSE fills: no system links where it hit to whether it is leaking, how big the hole is, and how long the crew has, per wall panel, within seconds. India will need exactly this as Gaganyaan leads to the Bharatiya Antariksh Station. BAS-01 is publicly described as roughly a 3.8 m × 8 m pressure vessel.

2 · The solution: every strike ends in one of three verdicts

GREEN
Shield absorbed
Only the Whipple bumper (the thin outer shield sheet) registered the strike. Logged to the impact map; no action needed.
AMBER
Hull struck, sealed
The pressure wall was hit at (x, y) with energy E, and the pressure is stable. Inspect at the next opportunity.
RED
Breach
Wall strike plus a pressure drop. Reports the hole location (x, y), the estimated hole diameter, and the time until a set pressure threshold.

What is new (the claim to defend)

"DIDS tells you something hit. Depressurisation analysers tell you how big the hole is, but only by compartment. HULLSENSE joins both at panel level. It times the strike on the bumper and the pressure wall, decides whether the shield absorbed it or the hull was hit, and sizes any resulting breach from the pressure decay. It self-calibrates from five taps and costs under ₹1,500."

  1. Two-layer triage. A sensor on the bumper and four on the wall separate "shield did its job" from "hull hit". DIDS on BEAM mounted its sensors only on the air barrier.
  2. Strike-to-breach fusion. A pressure-decay fit sizes the hole and attaches it to the most recent wall strike, giving location + size + time-to-threshold together.
  3. Hardware-timestamped arrival times without a fast ADC. The ESP32's MCPWM capture peripheral timestamps comparator edges at 12.5 ns, so the timing front end costs about ₹60 instead of a 1 MHz data-acquisition system.
  4. Energy from time-over-threshold. How long each comparator stays high, calibrated against ball drops of known energy (E = mgh).
  5. Self-calibration. Five taps at known points fit the plate's wave speed and each channel's timing offset.

3 · Technical approach

A. Locating the strike

  • Physics. A strike sends bending waves through the plate. In 1 mm aluminium they travel at only a few hundred m/s to ~900 m/s depending on frequency (build-risk judge's estimate). That is slow, so timing to a microsecond gives millimetre-scale resolution. A faster but tiny extensional wave arrives first, and a hard hit may trip the comparator on it. The band-pass stage before each comparator suppresses it (the TapTDOA hobby project hit the same issue).
  • Timing. The comparator edges go to the ESP32's MCPWM capture inputs. The ESP32 has 2 MCPWM units with 3 capture channels each, so 6 inputs; we use 5. They count the 80 MHz APB clock, a 12.5 ns tick, entirely in hardware, so Wi-Fi activity and interrupt delay don't add jitter. Capturing both edges also gives time-over-threshold.
  • Event gating. All four wall channels must fire within 1 ms (the longest possible plate transit plus margin), followed by a hold-off to ignore ringing. Footsteps and table thumps fail the gate. Rubber feet isolate the box.
  • Solver (runs in the browser, so firmware stays simple). Unknowns are the strike point (x, y) and the strike time t₀. The wave speed v and each channel's offset come from calibration.
    min over (x, y, t₀) of  Σi=1..4 [ ti − t₀ − |p − si| / v − bi ]²
    A coarse 2 mm grid search, then Gauss–Newton refinement. Four equations for three unknowns, so the leftover error doubles as a confidence score. Fallback: k-nearest-neighbour matching against arrival-time patterns from a 5×5 grid of recorded taps.
  • Self-calibration. Five taps at marked points; least squares fits v and the offsets b₁…b₄. Soft and hard taps at each point also fit an amplitude-dependent timing correction (bias as a function of time-over-threshold), which answers the hardest jury question in section 8.

B. Two-layer triage and energy

  • If the bumper sensor P5 fires first and the wall channels fire late and weak (the wave came through the standoffs), the verdict is GREEN. A direct wall strike gives a valid four-channel fit and the verdict is AMBER, unless the breach logic below upgrades it to RED.
  • Energy = k · f(Σ time-over-threshold, corrected for distance to each sensor). It is fitted against steel-ball drops from 5 / 10 / 20 / 40 cm (E = mgh) and reported in mJ with an uncertainty band.

C. Breach detection and sizing (the upgrade that makes it win)

The wall panel is the airtight lid of an ~8 L box, kept at ~1.5 kPa above room pressure through a tyre valve. Three drilled holes of 1, 2 and 3 mm are sealed with aluminium-foil patches. When a punch strike pierces a patch, air escapes through a real hole of known size.

  • Detect: dP/dt falls below a threshold for more than 0.3 s → a leak. It is linked to the most recent wall strike within 5 s → RED at (x, y). A leak with no recent strike is reported as "leak, location unknown: start a leak hunt", which is an honest result.
  • Size: fit the orifice blow-down model to the pressure curve for the effective hole area A, then take the diameter d = √(4A/π):
    dΔP/dt = −(P/V) · Cd · A · √(2ΔP/ρ)   (demo: small overpressure, subsonic flow)
    Computed: with an 8 L box at 2.5 kPa, the pressure falls in 10.7 s / 2.7 s / 1.2 s through 1 / 2 / 3 mm holes. That is long enough to see and fit at BMP280 rates. The diameter scales as √Cd, so if Cd is really anywhere from 0.60 to 0.70 the estimate is off by only −2% to +6%. We can claim diameter accuracy within about ±10% and verify it live against the drilled size.
  • Flight scaling (shown on the dashboard, clearly labelled as a model). In orbit air flows out to vacuum, so the flow is choked:
    P(t) = P₀ · e−t/τ,   τ = V / (Cd · A · c*),   c* = √(γRT)·(2/(γ+1))(γ+1)/2(γ−1) ≈ 199 m/s
    For a ~91 m³ module (BAS-01-class, approximate), the time until the module loses 10% of its pressure is 411 min through a 2 mm hole, 66 min through 5 mm, 16 min through 10 mm, and 3 min through 25 mm (computed). The crew-decision readout is the measured hole size combined with a configurable pressure threshold.

Firmware and dashboard

Firmware (firmware/hullsense_node): Arduino-ESP32 3.x sketch. Five MCPWM capture channels timestamp comparator edges at 12.5 ns; edges within 2 ms form one strike event; a 30 ms hold-off ignores ringing; the BMP280 streams box pressure at about 100 samples per second; a heartbeat reports per-channel edge counts so a dead sensor shows up as a fault. Builds for the ESP32 Dev Module with Arduino-ESP32 3.0.7 (317 KB flash, 21 KB RAM).

Dashboard (open it here): runs in Chrome or Edge and talks to the node over Web Serial. It runs the 5-tap calibration, locates strikes by least squares on the arrival times, decides the verdict, fits the pressure decay for the hole diameter and scales it to a station-class module. A clearly labelled simulation mode lets anyone try it without hardware.

Dashboard running in simulation mode (synthetic strikes). The solver test suite in tests/ checks calibration, localisation and hole sizing against simulated data.

7 · Questions we expect from the jury

They'll askYour answer
"A tap isn't a 7 km/s hypervelocity impact."Agreed. Never claim it is. This is a ground-test analogue that proves the localisation, triage and breach-sizing chain. Real hypervelocity impacts produce stronger, higher-frequency waves, which are easier to time. Validating with hypervelocity tests is future scope.
"Wave speed depends on frequency, and the comparator fires earlier on hard hits. How does error grow with drop height?"Show the measured soft-vs-hard bias from 8 Oct and the band-pass plus amplitude correction that fixes it.
"Isn't this just TapTDOA / DIDS?"We credit both. Our contribution: two-layer triage, strike-to-breach fusion with hole sizing, 12.5 ns hardware timestamps with no fast ADC, and self-calibration.
"Real modules are curved and ribbed."Per-panel calibration, with the fingerprint map as a fallback that learns any geometry. Ribs are future scope.
"Your hole-size estimate depends on Cd."The diameter goes as √Cd, so Cd anywhere from 0.60 to 0.70 changes it by only −2% to +6%. Then show the live check against the drilled size.
"Footsteps and crowd noise?"The 1 ms four-channel coincidence gate plus a residual cut-off. Show the false-event count from the 11 Oct soak.
